Signal or luck? (significance)

A return edge means little if it’s within the noise. This compares the manager’s fund (net of fee) to VTI month by month.

Over 121 months (~10 years), DFEOX beat VTI by -1.8%/yr after fee, with tracking error 2.3% (information ratio -0.79). The t-stat is -2.50 — large enough to suggest more than luck. And ~10 years is only a few independent annual observations, so read even a 'significant' result cautiously.

If cheap, buyable ETFs reproduce the manager’s fund, you keep the strategy and drop the fee. This fits DFEOX’s monthly returns with a low-cost ETF blend (best-fit weights over 81 months).

A third option: a flat-fee advisor

It isn’t only "manager vs DIY." A flat or hourly fiduciary charges the same dollars regardless of balance, so on a large portfolio it can cost a fraction of a percentage fee. Terminal wealth on $500,000 at 7.0%/yr under each cost structure.

A $4,000/yr flat fee equals a 1.4% fee once your balance passes $285,714. Above that, flat-fee advice beats %-of-assets — and the gap compounds.

What does the fee buy? (beyond returns)

The numbers above only see returns. A manager may add value the charts can’t — and the fee is worth it only if these clear the ~1.4%/yr gap — about $7,000/yr on a $500,000 balance. Treat this as a checklist for a fee-only fiduciary, not a verdict.

  • Financial & retirement planning — cash-flow, goals, withdrawal strategy
  • Tax management — tax-loss harvesting, asset location, Roth timing
  • Rebalancing & discipline — systematic, unemotional rebalancing
  • Behavioral coaching — staying invested through downturns — often the biggest one
  • Estate & insurance coordination — beneficiaries, trusts, coverage gaps

Behavioral coaching is the wild card: studies have estimated DIY investors give up ~1–2%/yr to mistimed buying and selling — which can rival the fee itself, for better or worse.
